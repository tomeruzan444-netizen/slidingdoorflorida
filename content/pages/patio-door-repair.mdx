## Which patio door do you have?

"Patio door" covers at least four different machines. A two-panel slider, a wall of stacking glass, a pocketing system and a pair of French doors all lead outside, but they wear out in different places. Find yours below, and you'll know where to look.

<PatioDoorTypes />

If you're not sure, open the door and watch the bottom edge. Wheels in a track mean a slider of some kind. Hinges on the side of the frame mean a swinging door, and the repairs happen at the hinges and the strike instead.

## On sliders, the bottom of the panel does the work

Two, three and four panel sliders all fail the same way, just on more panels. Every moving panel rides on rollers in its bottom rail, and when those wear, the panel sinks, drags and stops lining up with its lock. A stiff panel is usually a panel out of alignment, so that's the first thing to check.

On an OXXO door, the two center panels have to meet in the middle where they lock together. If one has dropped further than the other, the lock won't engage and the meeting edge gaps at the top. Leveling both panels to each other, not just to the frame, is what fixes it. Worn wheels themselves get [replaced and leveled](/sliding-door-roller-replacement), and a rough or dented rail is a job for [track repair](/sliding-door-track-repair).

## Multi-slide and pocketing doors are bigger versions of the same idea

Stacking and pocketing systems put three, four or more panels on parallel tracks. Each panel carries its own rollers, so each needs its own height adjustment. Marvin's installation instructions for its pocketing MultiSlide describe the process: lift the panel with a block of wood to take the weight off, open the adjustment cover, and set the height with a hex wrench until the gap along the frame is even.

The sill is the weak point. A multi-slide sill is wide and has to drain several tracks at once. Loewen's MultiSlide guide says its sills slope so water runs off through the weep system, and that sill pans must be used in every installation. Pocketing doors add a hidden problem, because whatever blows into the pocket stays there. Marvin's instructions call for the pocket itself to be panned with an end dam on every side, which tells you how seriously water in that cavity is taken.

If a multi-slide door leaks at the sill even with clean weep holes, the cause may be the installation under it, and that needs someone to look in person.

## Hinged French doors fail at the hinges and the strike

A French door that rubs at the top corner, or won't latch without a shove, has usually sagged on its hinges. JELD-WEN's patio door guide covers both kinds of fix. Adjustable hinges have screws you turn a quarter turn at a time to shift the panel. Standard hinges get thin shims behind the hinge plate to push the door back into square.

The latch side follows. When the door drops, the latch or the hooks of a multipoint lock miss the strike plate on the frame. The fix is to loosen the strike screws, slide the plate to meet the latch, and shim behind it if needed. Many French doors also have a passive panel held shut by bolts at the top and bottom, and those bolts stop reaching their holes once the doors sag.

Multipoint locks need cleaning on the coast. Blow the sand and salt out of the mechanism with compressed air, then use a waterproof lubricant.

## Glass is the same problem on every type

Fog between the panes means the sealed glass unit has failed, whatever the door style. A cracked pane needs safety glass made to size. Federal rules treat glass in patio-type sliding doors as Category II safety glazing, and glass in other doors moves into that same category once any single piece passes 9 square feet, which is easy to reach on a big French door. That means replacement glass for a large French door lite is held to the same standard as a slider's.

## Brand, model and the hunt for parts

On an older patio door, the repair is often the easy part. Finding the part is the job.

PGT and CGI both make impact doors in Florida, which is why their older models turn up all over Miami-Dade and Broward. So a patio door call starts with identification: the door type, the brand and model if a label survives, and measurements of the worn part if it doesn't. Then we work the door through its full movement, check the gaps around every panel, and look at the sill drainage. You'll hear which part is worn, whether it's adjustable or needs replacing, and whether parts for that door are still made.

## Weather in the frame, not just the panels

Patio doors keep weather out with weatherstrip, sweeps and the way panels overlap. When a slider's panel is set too high or too low, those seals stop meeting and air and water come through. Wet floors after a storm usually start at the sill, though, so read about weep holes on our [track repair](/sliding-door-track-repair) page before blaming the frame.

When the frame itself is rotted, corroded or bent, repair may not be worth it. Our [sliding glass door replacement](/sliding-glass-door-replacement) page covers that decision and Florida's permit rules. For a symptom-by-symptom approach to any slider, see [sliding glass door repair](/).
