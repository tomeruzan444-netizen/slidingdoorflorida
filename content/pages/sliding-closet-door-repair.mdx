## First, find out if it hangs or rolls

Bedroom closet sliders look simple, but they come in two designs that fail in opposite ways. On a **top-hung** door, wheels in the header track carry the weight and the floor guide only keeps the doors in line. On a bottom-rolling door, which covers most mirror doors, rollers in a floor track carry the weight and the top track only keeps the panel upright.

That one difference decides where you adjust the door and why it came off. Here's how to tell what you have, along with bifold and pocket doors for comparison.

## Why a closet door keeps jumping the track

On a bottom-rolling mirror door, the reason we find most often is that the door sits too low. The top of the panel needs to reach far enough up into the top channel to stay captured there. Renin's instructions for its mirror bypass doors call for raising each door at least 3/8 of an inch into the top track channel. A door that has settled below that pops out the first time someone bumps it.

Top-hung doors leave the track for different reasons: a hanger wheel that's cracked or worn, a hanger screw that has loosened so the door tilts, or a floor guide that's missing, so the bottom of the door swings and levers the wheels out. Many top tracks are shaped to hold the wheels in, so a door that keeps falling out of one usually has a problem at the hanger or the guide.

Some doors have anti-jump clips that snap into the track channel once the bottom roller is set to the right height. If they're broken or missing, the door has nothing holding it down.

## Adjusting the height

The adjustment is almost always a screw, but where it is depends on the design. On top-hung doors, the hangers on the back of each panel have slotted holes or a cam, and many adjust up or down without taking the screws out. On bottom-rolling mirror doors, each bottom roller has its own adjusting screw, reached from the edge or face of the frame near the floor.

Direction isn't consistent between brands. Renin's instructions say counterclockwise raises its doors, but don't assume that holds for yours. Turn a little, then look at the gap.

A gap between the door edge and the wall means the panel is out of plumb. Turn the screw on the wheel nearest the wall until the panel sits flush against it, raising one side or lowering the other until the edge lines up with the jamb from top to bottom.

## Putting a mirror door back on the track

Mirror doors are heavy and awkward, so this is a two-person job. Get the rear door in first. It goes in the back channel at the top and the back groove at the bottom, and the front door goes in front.

The usual method is to tip the bottom of the door out about 15 degrees, lift the top guides into the top track, then swing the bottom in and lower the rollers into the floor track. After that, raise the rollers until the door sits plumb and well up in the top channel.

If the rollers are cracked, flat-spotted or won't spin, replacing them is usually possible. Closet door rollers come in different diameters and edge shapes (flat, concave and convex), and some are brand-specific, so take one off and match it.

## Mirrors, safety backing and black edges

Treat a cracked mirror door gently. Most current mirror closet doors are built with a safety backing on the glass to meet the federal safety glazing standard. That backing is what holds broken glass together, so leave it in place, keep the panel upright in the track or lay it flat, and don't pick at loose pieces.

Black spots creeping in from the edges of the mirror are desilvering, not dirt. Moisture gets behind the reflective coating and eats it away, and nothing on the door adjusts it out. Homes near the water see it for a reason: Renin's warranty for its mirror doors excludes black edges, discoloration and slight hardware rusting when the doors are installed within five miles of salt water. Draw that five-mile line along the coast from Miami to Boca Raton and a lot of South Florida closets fall inside it. Once it spreads, the mirror or the panel needs replacing.

## Carpet, floor tracks and the closet that never quite closes

Bottom tracks don't belong on top of carpet. Cut the carpet back and fasten the track to the floor, or set thin wood strips under it so it has something solid to sit on. A track that sinks into pad flexes every time the door rolls over it, and the door drifts off line.

## This isn't a patio door repair, and that's good news

Closet doors are much lighter than patio sliders, they never see rain or salt directly, and they don't hold out weather. Many repairs come down to a screwdriver, a matched roller and some patience.
