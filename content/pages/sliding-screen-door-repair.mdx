## Fix the frame, the rollers or the mesh

A sliding screen door has three parts that fail, and they fail for different reasons. The mesh tears or goes brittle in the sun. The thin aluminum frame bends when it gets bumped or leaned on. And the little rollers at the bottom wear out until the screen drags, wobbles or falls off its track.

Most screen problems are one of the three, and sorting out which tells you whether you need a patch, a rescreen, new rollers or a new screen door altogether.

## Why the screen keeps coming off the track

A screen door that pops out at the bottom is almost always a roller problem. Screen rollers are small spring-loaded wheels, usually one or two at each bottom corner, and the spring pushes the wheel down so the screen stays snug between the top and bottom tracks. When a wheel cracks, a spring weakens, or the adjustment screw backs out, the screen loses that pressure and walks off the rail.

These aren't the same parts as the rollers under your glass door. They're smaller, lighter and sold separately, so measure the old ones (wheel diameter and housing size) before buying. If the glass panel is the one dragging, that's a heavier job covered on our [roller replacement](/sliding-door-roller-replacement) page.

A bent bottom rail on the screen frame does the same thing, and so does a dented screen track. Sight down the frame from one end. If it bows, the screen won't sit level no matter what the rollers do.

## Patch, rescreen or replace?

Small, clean holes can take a patch kit. They're not pretty, but they keep bugs out.

Rescreening makes sense when the frame is straight and its corners are tight. The old mesh and spline (the rubber cord that holds the mesh in its groove) come out, new mesh is stretched across, and new spline is rolled in. If the mesh crumbles when you press it with a thumb, it's sun-damaged all over and patching won't last.

A new screen door is the better buy when the frame is bent, the corners have split, or the whole screen has been off the track so many times that the rails are chewed up. Rescreening a bent frame just gives you a tight new mesh on a door that still won't slide.

## Choosing mesh for a Florida door

This is where Florida screens differ from screens anywhere else. The mesh you pick matters as much as the repair.

For homes near salt water, Phifer, one of the large screen makers, recommends fiberglass, its sun control screens, and its tougher TuffScreen and PetScreen products in its [screening FAQ](https://www.phifer.com/screening/faq/). Aluminum mesh isn't on that list.

No-see-ums need a straight answer. Phifer sells a 20 by 20 mesh for them, and it does stop most tiny insects. A University of Arizona extension sheet on biting midges notes they can get through standard 16 by 16 and 18 by 16 screen, and it suggests 30 by 30 mesh where you can manage it. A 20 by 20 screen is a real improvement over standard, but it isn't a wall, and it costs some breeze.

Solar screen is worth it on doors that face west or south. Phifer's SunTex blocks 80 or 90 percent of the sun's heat and glare, depending on which grade you choose. The trade-off is the view: the 90 percent version lets through only about a fifth of the visible light, so the screen reads as a dark panel from inside during the day.

Pet screen is a vinyl-coated polyester built to resist claws and paws. It's thicker than regular mesh, which matters at install time, because thicker mesh needs a thinner spline to fit the same groove.

## Spline size, the detail that ruins rescreens

Spline comes in sizes measured in thousandths of an inch, and a mismatch is a common reason a DIY rescreen pulls loose or won't go in. Phifer's sizing chart lists .140 inch spline for standard fiberglass in common grooves, .125 inch for aluminum mesh, and one size smaller for pet and solar screens.

The surest way to choose is to pull a piece of the old spline and match it, then go down a size if you're switching to a thicker mesh. To check what mesh you have now, Phifer suggests cutting a one-inch paper square and counting the openings across and down.

## What a screen visit covers

We check the screen track and the rollers before touching the mesh, because a new screen on a bad rail won't stay put. Then we look at the frame: straight and square means rescreen, bent or split means a new frame.

If you want a different mesh, bring it up. Switching from standard to pet screen or solar screen happens at the same time as the rescreen.
