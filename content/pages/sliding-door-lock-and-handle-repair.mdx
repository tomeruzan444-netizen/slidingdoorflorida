## Why the latch stopped catching

Before you buy a lock, lift the handle end of the door slightly while you push the latch closed. If it catches with that extra bit of height, the lock isn't the problem. The door has dropped.

Locks are one of the three problems we see most, and our founder's description covers both causes:

<JosephSays>
  <p>Locking mechanisms also wear out or become misaligned.</p>
</JosephSays>

A sliding door latch is a hook on the moving panel that swings into a keeper (also called a strike) mounted on the frame. The two have to line up within a small margin. When the rollers under the panel wear or settle, the panel sinks, and the hook ends up swinging under the keeper instead of into it.

The fix for that is raising the panel with its [roller adjustment screws](/sliding-door-roller-replacement), then fine-tuning the keeper. Most keepers sit in slotted screw holes, so loosening two screws lets you slide it up or down to meet the hook. Skip that second step and a perfectly leveled door can still refuse to lock.

Replace the lock itself when the hook is bent or snapped, the lever inside the handle spins without moving the hook, or the latch is so loose it rattles. Those are worn parts, and no adjustment brings them back.

## Mortise locks and surface-mount handles

Sliding door hardware comes in two basic layouts, and the replacement has to match the one you have.

With a **mortise lock**, the latch mechanism is a separate metal body that slides into a pocket cut into the edge of the panel, held by two screws through a faceplate. The handle on the face of the door carries only the lever that works it. With a surface-mount handle set, the hook is built into the handle itself and the whole assembly sits on the inside face.

The two parts work as a system. Retail listings for handle sets often note which lock types they're built for, and many mortise-style handle sets are sold without the lock body inside. A handle that fits your screw holes can still fail to work your lock if the lever and the keyway angle don't match.

## Measure before you order

The single measurement that matters most for a handle is the distance between its mounting screws, center to center. Get that and the panel thickness right, and most of the guesswork is gone.

If your door is older or an unusual brand, take photos of both faces of the handle, the edge of the panel with the faceplate, and the keeper. A good parts counter can often match from photos when the measurements land between sizes.

## The layers that actually secure a slider

A latch keeps the door closed. On its own it doesn't do much to keep someone out. The St. Petersburg Police Department's [burglary prevention advice](https://police.stpete.org/crimePreventionTips/residentialBurglaryPrevention.html) says it directly: sliding glass doors "are secured by latches rather than locks," and many older sliders can be lifted up and off their track.

Real security comes from adding layers that work even if the latch is beaten:

- A blocking device in the bottom track, such as a fold-down security bar, a screw-down track blocker, or a dowel cut to length
- Screws driven partway into the upper track above the sliding panel, or anti-lift blocks, so the panel can't be raised off its rollers
- A pin lock through both panels where they overlap
- Rollers adjusted so the panel isn't sagging, which the St. Petersburg police list as part of keeping a door from being lifted out

Anti-lift screws need care. Set them too low and the door binds against them every time it slides. They should leave just enough clearance for the panel to move freely.

For forced-entry ratings on a new door, ASTM F842 is the test method used for sliding door assemblies. If security is the main reason you're shopping, asking whether a door has been tested to it is a fair question, and it's one reason some owners move to a [new door with a multipoint lock](/sliding-glass-door-replacement).

## Pool homes and Florida's door alarm option

If a slider opens straight onto your pool area, Florida's pool safety law has something to say about it. Section 515.27 of the [Florida Statutes](http://www.leg.state.fl.us/statutes/index.cfm?App_mode=Display_Statute&URL=0500-0599/0515/Sections/0515.27.html) requires a new residential pool to have at least one of five safety features before it passes final inspection. Two of them involve doors.

One is an exit alarm on every door and window with direct access to the pool, rated at least 85 dB A at 10 feet. The other is a self-closing, self-latching device on those doors, with its release at least 54 inches above the floor. Sliding glass doors don't close themselves, so the alarm is the route most slider owners see.

Whether and how this applies to your existing home is a question for your local building department. We won't give legal advice on it. What we can tell you is that an alarm sensor is another piece of hardware on the door, and it only works properly on a panel that closes fully and lines up.
