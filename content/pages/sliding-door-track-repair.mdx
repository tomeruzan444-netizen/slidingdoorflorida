## Clean, straighten, cap or replace

Look closely at the rail your door rolls on before you decide anything. Most tracks need one of four things, and they go from cheapest to most involved: a proper cleaning, a careful straightening, a stainless cap over the worn rail, or a new section of sill. ## Getting sand and salt out properly

A vacuum with a crevice tool does most of the work. Run it along both channels, then go after what's left. Loewen's coastal maintenance guide recommends [compressed air for sand and salt](https://www.loewen.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Maintenance-of-Loewen-Products-1.pdf) in tracks, which reaches the corners a brush can't. Finish with a damp cloth and let the sill dry.

Skip the harsh cleaners. Vinegar-based, citrus and abrasive products are hard on hardware finishes.

Lubricant is where door makers openly disagree:

## Weep holes keep the sill from flooding

The small slots in the bottom of the track are drains. Rain that gets into the sill is supposed to collect there and run out to the exterior through weep holes. If that water can't drain, it finds its way into the floor and the wall around the door instead.

Testing takes a cup of water:

1. Pour about a cup into the inside channel of the sill.
2. Go outside and watch the weep holes on the outer face. Water should run out.
3. If it doesn't, push a pipe cleaner or thin wire gently into the outside hole to break up the clog.
4. Don't force the wire deep, since it can damage the sealed glass unit.
5. Pour again until the water runs clear.

The Department of Energy's [Building America guide on weep holes](https://basc.pnnl.gov/diy-guides/clean-window-weep-holes) adds that compressed air helps, and that the goal is to pull debris out, not push it further in.

Two habits cause a lot of flooded sills. One is caulking the weep holes shut to "stop a leak," which traps the water inside instead. The other is pressure washing the door, which can push water into the sill faster than the weeps can drain it.

Storms test all of this. The Florida Building Commission's [2024 research report](https://www.floridabuilding.org/fbc/publications/Research_2024-2025/Final%20Report%20-%20Reviewing%20the%20Standards%20for%20Wind-Driven%20Rain%20Through%20Tracks%20of%20Sliding%20Glass%20Doors%20systems.pdf) on rain driven through sliding door tracks quotes FEMA inspectors who found some doors "lack adequate sill risers," the raised inner part of the sill that holds back wind-pushed water. A committee member in the same report stressed keeping weep holes open so water that gets in can get out. A sill that overflows even with clear weeps may be one of those low-riser doors, and that's a problem for someone to look at in person.

## When a track cap is the right fix

A track cap is a thin stainless sleeve that fits over the existing rail. It gives worn, grooved or dinged rails a new smooth top for the rollers.

Prime-Line's install sheet for its stainless cap shows the method. Remove any old cover, clean the track, run a thin bead of RTV silicone adhesive along the rail, set the cap on top, and tap it down with a block of wood about 14 to 16 inches long, moving along an inch every couple of hits. The caps come in 6, 8 and 10 foot lengths.

Three things to know before buying one:

- The cap needs the original rail underneath. If the rail is cracked through or missing, a cap has nothing to hold.
- Rails aren't all the same size. Measure the height and width of yours and compare it to the cap's opening.
- Once it's bonded down, plan on it staying there.

A cap also raises the rail slightly, so the rollers usually need a small height adjustment afterward. If they're worn too, [replacing the rollers](/sliding-door-roller-replacement) at the same time saves a second visit.

## Bent rails and broken rails

A rail knocked sideways by a dropped chair or a dolly can often be eased back into line, but only gently. Aluminum that gets bent back and forth too much cracks.

A rail that's split or broken off needs a new piece of track or a new sill. On some doors that's a section that screws in. On others the rail is part of one long aluminum sill extrusion, and replacing it means taking the frame apart. If the sill is corroded across its whole length or the frame has twisted out of square, the honest conversation moves toward [replacing the door](/sliding-glass-door-replacement).

## That dark residue on bare aluminum

Gray or black smudges along an unpainted rail are oxidation, and on a door near the Intracoastal or the beach they come back faster. Bare aluminum edges and the area around the weep holes show it first, and the residue wipes off with a fine scratch pad. Light oxidation is cosmetic. Deep pitting on top of the rail is what grinds roller wheels, and that's the point where a cap earns its keep.

## Leaks that aren't the track

Some water problems come from the frame around the door rather than the sill. Worn weatherstrip, a gap at the jamb, or a hinged door that no longer seals all show up as wet floors. Our [patio door repair](/patio-door-repair) page covers those. For every other symptom, the [sliding glass door repair](/) guide starts with what the door is doing and works back to the cause.
