# Content guide: Sliding Glass Door Repair website (Florida)

> Every piece of content on the site, now and in the future (service pages, city pages, articles, FAQs, microcopy), is written according to this guide.
> All content is in **American English** (color, aluminum, center).
> This guide is based on Google Search Central documentation (helpful content, spam policies, AI-generated content, AI features), the Search Quality Rater Guidelines, and catalogs of AI writing signs (Wikipedia's WikiProject AI Cleanup and Pangram's frequency research).

---

## Part A: What Google requires, in practice

### 1. The core rule: people-first
Google rewards content created to help people, not to manipulate rankings. The test: **if a visitor landed on this page directly, without Google, would they leave with what they need to act?**

Questions every page must pass (based on Google's self-assessment questions):
- Does it contain **original** information, analysis, or experience, not just a summary of what's already online?
- Does it cover the topic fully enough that the visitor won't need to go back and search again?
- Does it offer insight beyond the obvious?
- Does the title describe the content accurately, without exaggeration?
- Would someone bookmark it or send it to a neighbor whose door is stuck?
- Does it contain easily verifiable factual errors?
- Is it clear who is behind the content and why they should be trusted?

### 2. Warning signs of search-engine-first content (avoid entirely)
- Writing to a target word count. **Google has no preferred length.** A page is exactly as long as the topic requires.
- Producing many similar pages hoping some will rank (scaled content abuse).
- City pages or variations that funnel to the same destination (doorway abuse).
- Changing page dates without substantive changes.
- Summarizing other sources without adding value.
- Promising an answer to a question that has no real answer.

### 3. E-E-A-T: experience, expertise, authoritativeness, trust
- **Trust matters most.** Real data, a real license, real people.
- **Experience:** Google looks for evidence of first-hand involvement. For us that means: what we've seen in Florida homes, how the failure looks and sounds, what we found when we took it apart, and photos from real jobs.
- **Strictly forbidden:** invented author profiles, AI-generated headshots, credentials or licenses that don't exist, fake reviews, or "customer stories" that never happened. Google explicitly calls this deception and a low-quality signal.

### 4. Main content
Per the Quality Rater Guidelines, the quality of the main content is the most critical factor in page quality. It's judged on:
- **Effort:** human work invested. Bulk AI text without editing and oversight counts as little to no effort.
- **Originality:** information or perspectives not available on other sites.
- **Skill:** clear writing, working tools and infographics.
- **Accuracy:** factual correctness. Safety topics (glass, hurricanes, locks) require especially high accuracy.

### 5. Using AI
Google doesn't ban AI use. It bans mass-producing pages without added value. Language models predict likely word sequences rather than retrieving facts, so they can be wrong ("hallucinate"). Therefore:
- **Every fact is checked against a source** before publishing.
- Every page gets human editing that adds the business's real experience.
- AI helps with research and structure. The experience, opinions, and details belong to the business.

### 6. AI search (AI Overviews, AI Mode, ChatGPT, Perplexity)
- Google: no special requirements, it's regular SEO. What matters most is **non-commodity content**: field experience and an expert's perspective instead of yet another generic list.
- No need to split content into artificial "chunks" or write "for AI". These systems understand synonyms and intent.
- What actually helps:
  - **Answer-first:** the answer in the first sentence, context after.
  - **Quotable sentences:** a clear claim with a specific detail ("Most stuck doors we see in coastal Broward homes have rollers worn flat on one side, not a damaged track").
  - **Numbers with conditions**, not generalizations.
  - **Clear entities:** business name, Florida, service type, consistent everywhere.

## Part B: The site's voice

### 7. Who is talking
An experienced technician explaining to a Florida homeowner what's going on with their door, like a conversation standing next to the door. Direct, practical, and honest, including saying "you can do this yourself" or "this isn't worth repairing".

- **First person plural** ("we", "our techs") about the business, and **"you"** to the visitor.
- **Natural contractions:** don't, it's, you'll.
- **Register:** plain, professional, not academic. A trade term (mortise lock, weep holes, tempered glass) gets a one-sentence explanation the first time it appears.
- **Opinion:** allowed and encouraged ("We'd rather replace rollers than recommend a new door when the frame is solid").
- **Honesty about limits:** what we don't do, when it's expensive, when something else is a better option.

### 8. What makes text human and authentic
- **Details only someone who did the work would know:** the sound of a worn roller, what a salt-eaten track looks like, how long a typical job takes, what people get wrong.
- **Specific numbers** (from the owner or a source): measurements, times, price ranges with conditions.
- **Real Florida:** salt air near the coast, sand in tracks, humidity, strong sun, hurricane season (June 1 to November 30), HVHZ areas, condo buildings with HOA rules.
- **Trade-offs instead of promises:** "Stainless rollers cost more and last longer near salt water. Inland, nylon is usually fine."
- **Varied rhythm:** A short sentence. Then a longer one that explains the context and the reason. Not every paragraph the same length.
- **Real job stories** (from the owner questionnaire), short and concrete.

## Part C: Blocklist of AI writing signs

> Sources: WikiProject AI Cleanup (Wikipedia), frequency research (Pangram: em dashes appear ~10x more often in AI text than human text, bullet lists ~9x, triads ~4x, "not just X but Y" ~3x), and analyses by Fast Company, Decrypt, and Search Engine Land.
> Important: individual words aren't the main problem. **Structural patterns** are the strongest and most persistent signal. This list doesn't replace good writing. It's a checking tool.

### 9. Punctuation and formatting
| Forbidden | Instead |
|---|---|
| Em dash (—) | **Zero on the entire site.** Comma, period, parentheses, or colon. Regular hyphen (-) only inside compound words. |
| Heavy bolding (bold in every paragraph) | Bold for one essential term per section, at most. |
| Title Case headings | Sentence case: "How to tell if your rollers are worn" |
| Emojis | Never. |
| Bullets in every section | Bullets only for real lists (steps, parts, comparison). Most content is prose. |
| Repeated "**Label:** explanation" bullets | Prose or a table. |
| Colons in every heading ("Rollers: What You Need to Know") | A direct heading. |

### 10. Structural patterns (the strongest signal)
- **Negative parallelism:** "It's not just a door, it's your home's first line of defense." / "It's less about X and more about Y." / "Not only... but also." **Forbidden.**
- **Automatic triads:** "fast, reliable, and affordable" / "Repair, restore, and protect." At most one triad per page, and only when there are exactly three things.
- **Trailing -ing clauses:** "...replacing the rollers, ensuring smooth operation for years to come." / "..., highlighting the importance of maintenance." **Forbidden.** Write a new sentence with a subject and verb.
- **Generic openers:** "When it comes to sliding glass doors..." / "In today's fast-paced world..." / "Sliding glass doors are an essential part of any home." **Forbidden.** Open with the problem or the answer.
- **Rhetorical-question transitions:** "The catch?" / "The result?" / "So what does this mean for you?" **Forbidden.**
- **Section-ending summaries:** "In summary," / "In conclusion," / "Overall," / "Ultimately," **Forbidden.**
- **"Whether you're... or...":** "Whether you're a homeowner or a property manager..." **Forbidden.**
- **Symmetrical paragraphs:** every paragraph exactly 3 sentences, every section the same structure. **Break it on purpose.**
- **"Perfect world":** everything works flawlessly and every customer is satisfied. **Forbidden.** Mention limits and risks too.
- **Vague attribution:** "Experts say..." / "Studies show..." / "Many homeowners find..." **Forbidden** without a specific source.
- **Stacked hedging:** "may potentially help to possibly reduce". One claim with one real caveat.
- **Puffery:** "stands as a testament", "plays a vital role", "underscores the importance". **Forbidden.**
- **"serves as" / "stands as" / "represents"** instead of "is". Write "is".

### 11. Forbidden words and phrases
**Verbs:** delve, leverage, elevate, unlock, unleash, empower, embark, navigate (metaphorical), foster, streamline, revolutionize, transform (marketing sense), harness, boast, showcase, underscore, tailor (marketing sense), ensure (overuse: once per page at most).

**Adjectives:** seamless, seamlessly, robust, comprehensive, cutting-edge, state-of-the-art, top-notch, world-class, unparalleled, unmatched, second to none, innovative, meticulous, vibrant, nestled, bustling, pivotal, crucial (overuse), essential (overuse), ever-evolving, game-changing, hassle-free, stress-free, worry-free, premier, trusted (without evidence).

**Nouns and phrases:** tapestry, realm, landscape (metaphorical), journey, testament, peace of mind, one-stop shop, your trusted partner, look no further, rest assured, we pride ourselves, we go above and beyond, we've got you covered, it's important to note, it's worth noting, in today's world, at the end of the day, a wide range of, in the realm of, dive into, deep dive, game-changer, the bottom line.

**Overused transitions:** moreover, furthermore, additionally, consequently, notably, indeed. One per page at most, preferably zero.

### 12. Empty marketing claims specific to this trade
- "Restore your door to like-new condition" → say what's actually done: "New rollers and a cleaned track usually bring a door back to one-finger sliding."
- "Our expert technicians" → who they are, and what their experience is.
- "Fast and affordable service" → real response time and a price range with conditions.
- "Quality you can trust" → license, insurance, warranty (real ones).

## Part D: Page structure and writing

### 13. Before writing: a mandatory brief
Every page gets `/briefs/{slug}.md` with:
1. **Search intent:** what the visitor is trying to accomplish and what situation they're in (door stuck right now? planning a replacement? looking for a part?).
2. **SERP review:** what the top 5-10 results cover, what's missing, and what we'll add that isn't there.
3. **The unique angle** in one sentence.
4. **Facts and sources:** every technical or regulatory claim, with a link to a primary source.
5. **Business experience:** what from the owner questionnaire goes on the page.
6. **Infographic:** type and information.
7. **FAQ list**, checked against `faq-registry.json`.
8. **Internal links:** where the page links to, and which pages will link to it.

### 14. Uniqueness across pages
- **Every page opens differently.** No two pages share an opening sentence, opening pattern, or section order.
- **Different H2s.** No "Why Choose Us", "Our Process", and "Benefits of X" on every page. If a process section is needed, its heading and content are specific to the service ("What a roller swap looks like on-site").
- **No shared blocks in body content.** Repeated text (service area, business strengths) lives only in the header, footer, and sidebar, never in the page body.
- **A different infographic on every page**, both in type (table, diagram, decision tree, measuring guide) and in content.
- **The swap test:** if you can swap the service name for another service and the text still makes sense, it's too generic. Rewrite it.
- Automated similarity check: maximum 15% 5-gram overlap between any two pages.

### 15. Text structure
- **Short paragraphs:** 1-4 sentences, roughly 60 words max per paragraph.
- **Sentences:** average 12-18 words, with real variation (5 words and 25 words both appear).
- **Answer-first:** every H2 phrased as a question is answered in the first sentence beneath it.
- **Bullets:** only for real lists, 2-3 lists per page max, 3-7 items each.
- **Tables:** for comparisons (roller types, mesh types, repair vs. replace).
- **An H2 every 150-300 words**, H3 only for a real subtopic.
- **Readability:** target Flesch Reading Ease of 60-70 (roughly grades 7-9).

### 16. Keyword integration
- **Primary keyword:** in the title, H1, first 100 words, at least one H2 (phrased naturally), URL, and meta description. **Beyond that, only where natural.** No density target.
- **Secondary cluster keywords:** each appears once or twice, in a context where it genuinely fits.
- **"Near me" terms are never written as "near me" in the text.** Write clearly about the Florida service area instead.
- **Synonyms and natural language:** slider, patio slider, glass door, door panel, but never forced.
- **Read-aloud test:** if a sentence sounds odd read aloud because of a keyword, rewrite it.

### 17. FAQ
- 8-15 questions per page, and no question repeats on another page.
- **Question sources:** People also ask, related searches, Reddit (r/HomeImprovement, r/florida), Houzz, and questions the owner hears on the phone.
- **The question** phrased the way a person would type or say it ("Can I replace sliding door rollers myself?").
- **The answer:** a first sentence that answers directly (Yes / No / About X / Do Y), then 1-2 sentences of caveat or detail. 25-60 words total.
- **Don't** restate the question in the answer ("Great question! Sliding door rollers can...").
- Price questions: a range with conditions, only if the owner approved it. Otherwise explain what drives the price.

### 18. Titles and meta
- **Title:** up to ~60 characters, primary keyword first, then a differentiator (Florida / brand name). No "Best", no "#1", no exclamation marks.
- **Meta description:** up to ~155 characters. What the visitor gets on the page, in one or two human sentences.
- **H1:** includes the primary keyword, descriptive, not salesy.

### 19. Infographics
- Built in HTML/SVG with real text, never an image with baked-in text.
- Each infographic answers one question the visitor actually has (what do I have? what fits me? what's the next step?).
- `figure` + a `figcaption` that states the takeaway in text.
- Accessibility: contrast, appropriate alt/aria, and correct reading in screen readers and on mobile.

## Part E: Sources and accuracy

### 20. Preferred (primary) sources
- **Florida Building Code** and **Florida Product Approval:** floridabuilding.org
- **Miami-Dade Product Control (NOA)** for HVHZ areas
- **Florida DBPR:** contractor licensing and license verification (myfloridalicense.com)
- **County and city permit offices** (for city pages)
- **CPSC, 16 CFR 1201:** safety glazing in doors
- **NFRC:** energy performance ratings
- **FGIA (formerly AAMA):** window and door standards
- **NOAA / National Hurricane Center:** hurricane season
- **Manufacturer instructions** (installation, maintenance, parts)
- **The business's own experience:** the owner questionnaire and job stories

### 21. Accuracy rules
- Every regulatory fact is checked against the official source at the time of writing (codes and rules change). Record the check date in the brief.
- When in doubt, say so honestly: "Permit rules vary by city. We check with your local building department before any replacement."
- **No legal or insurance advice.** Explain principles and refer to the right party.
- External links: only to primary, reliable sources, when they help the visitor (e.g. license verification on DBPR). No links to competitors.

### 22. Never invent
Customer names, reviews, quotes, business statistics ("97% of our customers..."), years in business, number of jobs, licenses, warranties, arrival times, prices, stock "before/after" photos presented as the business's work. **All of these come only from the owner.** Until then, a placeholder stays.

## Part F: Pre-publish checklist

- [ ] A complete brief with sources exists.
- [ ] The angle is unique, and the page passes the swap test.
- [ ] At least one real experience detail from the business (or a marked placeholder).
- [ ] Zero em dashes, no blocklisted words or patterns (linter passed).
- [ ] Overlap with every other page is under 15%.
- [ ] 8-15 FAQs, no duplicates against the registry.
- [ ] Answer block at the top, and answer-first under every question H2.
- [ ] A unique infographic, accessible and readable on mobile.
- [ ] Primary keyword in the title, H1, first 100 words, one H2, URL, and meta.
- [ ] 2+ outbound contextual links, and 2+ inbound from relevant pages.
- [ ] Sidebar shows only related services.
- [ ] Every fact checked against a source.
- [ ] Nothing invented about the business.
- [ ] Read-aloud test: it sounds like someone who knows the work talking to a neighbor.
