## 1. Search intent

Three overlapping situations:

1. **The door won't lock right now.** The latch hook doesn't catch, the lever moves but nothing happens, or the handle snapped. The visitor wants to know if it's the lock, the handle, or the door itself, and whether it's a quick fix. Many are worried about leaving the house unsecured tonight.
2. **Buying a part.** "Patio door lock replacement" and "sliding door handle replacement" searchers are often standing in front of the door with a tape measure or are about to order a handle set online. They need to know what to measure (hole spacing, lock style, keyway angle, door thickness) so they don't buy the wrong part.
3. **Security upgrade.** After a break-in nearby, a move into a new home, or a vacation, they want to know how to make the slider harder to open or lift out. In Florida, some also have a pool behind the slider and want to know about door alarms and child safety.

Visitor state: mostly homeowners and landlords, some property managers. Mixed DIY and hire-out intent.

**Gaps we fill:**
- One clear way to tell **mortise-style vs surface-mount (hook-in-handle) systems** before buying.
- A **measuring guide** that covers both the handle (center-to-center spacing, door thickness) and the mortise lock (mounting-hole spacing, keyway angle, faceplate end shape), with the common sizes in one place.
- The diagnosis order: **check door height and keeper before buying a lock**, since a sagged door is a common reason the hook misses (link to roller page, don't repeat it).
- Security **layers** explained as a system (latch, secondary blocking, anti-lift), with the trade-off that extra keyed locks and bars can conflict with emergency escape if the slider is a bedroom's escape opening.
- **Florida pool homes:** what Florida Statutes ch. 515 actually says about doors with direct pool access (exit alarm or self-closing/self-latching device), stated accurately, with no legal advice.

## 3. Unique angle (one sentence)

Before you buy a new patio door lock, find out whether the lock is really broken or the door has dropped, then measure the right three numbers so the replacement fits, and add security in layers that still let your family get out (and, on Florida pool homes, keep kids from getting out to the water).

### Part A: "Layers of sliding door security" diagram

Front-view schematic of a sliding panel in its frame (interior side), with numbered callouts from outermost "everyday" layer to added layers. Exact content:

1. **Latch (mortise lock or handle hook) + keeper.** Label: "Layer 1: the latch. Keeps the door shut. Works only if the door sits at the right height and the keeper lines up." Callout on the panel edge/jamb.
2. **Keyed cylinder (optional).** Label: "Keyed lock: locks the latch from outside. Some handle sets convert with a cylinder kit." Callout on exterior handle.
3. **Blocking device in the track.** Label: "Layer 2: stop the slide. Security bar (charley bar), track blocker, or cut dowel in the bottom track." Callout at mid-height bar and bottom track.
4. **Foot lock / pin lock.** Label: "Pin or foot lock: pins the sliding panel to the fixed panel or the sill so it can't slide." Callout at the meeting stiles / bottom rail. (Pin lock: drill placement must avoid the glass and latch; note.)
5. **Anti-lift in the header.** Label: "Layer 3: stop the lift. Screws or anti-lift blocks in the upper track above the sliding panel, set so the door still slides but can't be raised off the track." Callout at head track.
6. **Rollers adjusted.** Label: "Rollers set correctly. A sagging door is easier to lift and the latch misses the keeper." Small callout at bottom rail (link to roller page).
7. **Alarm sensor (Florida pool homes).** Label: "Door exit alarm on doors that open to a pool area: Florida law option of 85 dBA at 10 feet (s. 515.27, F.S.)." Callout at top corner.

Side note box on the figure: "Bedroom slider used as an escape route? Every layer must open from inside by hand, no key or tool." (Pending E1 confirmation for FBC; otherwise phrase as general safety advice.)

Figcaption (takeaway, draft direction only): the latch alone isn't security; block the slide, block the lift, and keep the door at the right height.

### Part B: Hole-spacing measuring instructions

Panel showing an interior handle with two screw holes and a tape measure, plus a small edge view of a mortise lock.

**Handle (step list):**
1. Remove or look behind the interior handle and find the two mounting screws.
2. Measure from the **center of the top screw hole to the center of the bottom screw hole** (center-to-center). Do not measure edge to edge or the handle's overall length.
3. Compare with common sizes: **3-15/16", 4-15/16", 5", 6-1/2", 6-5/8"** (also 3-1/2" exists). If yours is between sizes, re-measure; spacings are usually exact.
4. Measure **door panel thickness** where the handle mounts (inside face to outside face) and check it falls within the new set's listed range.
5. Note: mortise-style (separate lock body in the door edge) or surface-mount (hook in the handle); keyed or not; offset or centered thumb latch. Take a photo of the old parts.

**Mortise lock (step list):**
1. Remove the two screws on the panel edge and slide the lock body out.
2. Measure **mounting-hole spacing** on the faceplate, center to center (examples seen: 3-11/16", 3-7/8", 4-5/8", 5-3/8").
3. Note the **keyway angle** (45 degree vs vertical/90 degree when locked) and the **faceplate ends** (rounded or square).
4. Bring the old lock or a photo with measurements when buying.

Sources: L1-L7.
